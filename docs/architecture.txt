Profiling Application — Product & Architecture
Overview
1. The problem with the resume
As part of our suite of education applications, we are building a new-age profiling application.
We believe the current method, means, and — most importantly — metrics of representing an
individual do not reflect the true person in the right context.
For higher-education students, profiling today collapses to a resume. There is very little variance
between individuals, and most resumes fail to capture who the person actually is. In the race to
produce ATS-aligned resumes, students have forfeited their personality and converged on
near-identical representations of themselves, when in reality people are unique and widely
different in their skills and their approach to life.
A resume was also designed to capture history — a person's top five achievements. But today's
individuals are far more than their history. Their abilities, motives, inspirations, and skills are
highly complex, and representing that complexity properly gives them what a resume never can:
the right alignment in any matchmaking process.
When a company shortlists candidates, the ability to understand whether someone is a
high-agency or high-compliance individual, an extrovert or an introvert, lets it make the best
choice for both the company and the candidate. That is only scratching the surface — the
company might also see that a candidate is highly punctual, with that signal verified by the
mentors who supervised them over the course of their education. The principle throughout is
that we surface the aggregated metric — agency, impact, reliability — and never force the
company to scramble through hundreds of raw signals and infer for themselves.
2. Design principles
Five ideas anchor the system:
-
-
-
Multi-source. Insight comes from data, and data can come from anywhere. Our other
apps (project-based learning, mentorship) integrate as first-class sources, but any
external system can also plug in.
Aggregated, inferred metrics. We turn disparate raw data into intelligent, inferred,
human-readable metrics — and present those, not the raw material.
Credentials at the core. Data is only as valuable as it is verifiable. Credentialing is built
into the data source, not bolted on afterward.
-
-
Context is everything. Every metric is captured in a context. A profile should never be
read in isolation of the context it was earned in. We call that context a reward system.
Transposition. Because every individual has a distinct profile in every reward system,
the app's defining capability is to reliably translate a profile from one reward system into
another.
The end-user experience should be peppy and real, simple and derived. It should never feel like
a monitoring system — it should feel like a fitness application.
3. The layered architecture
The heart of the system is a vertical stack of abstraction. Data enters near the bottom as atomic
fact and rises into context-bound, comparable insight. Reading bottom-up:
Canonical trait basis ← shared interlingua; the transposition hub
▲
Reward system (lens) ← context: what is valued, and how it is weighted
▲
Metrics ← aggregated traits (agency, reliability, impact)
▲
Signals ← first-order insights ("submitted 2 days early")
▲
Derivation layer ← reusable, versioned transforms
▲
Observations ← atomic facts ("deadline"
,
"submitted date")
▲
Connectors ← multi-source, credentialed, context-tagged
3.1 The interpretation ladder: observations → derivations → signals →
metrics
The lowest meaningful distinction in the system is between a fact and an interpretation.
An observation is an atomic fact from a source, carrying no judgment beyond its own value:
deadline = Mar 5, submitted = Mar 3, commit_at = 23:14, meeting_attended =
true. Observations are captured within a context and carry source, timestamp, and a data-level
attestation.
A signal is the first-order insight derived from one or more observations —
"submitted 2 days
early.
" This is the first thing in the stack that means something, and it earns its name precisely
for that reason.
Between the two sits the derivation layer: a library of small, reusable, versioned transforms that
turn raw facts into insights. A rule such as "days relative to deadline" consumes a submission
date and a deadline date and emits the submitted_early signal. Derivations belong to the
app, not to any one connector, so a single rule is written once and reused across every source
that emits the relevant observations. They are versioned because the logic can be wrong and
later improved — and that version is part of the provenance every signal carries.
Signals then aggregate into metrics — the human-readable, context-bound traits we actually
surface, such as reliability, agency, and impact.
This is a directed graph, not a straight chain. One observation can feed many signals (a
submission date with a deadline yields "submitted early"; the same date with a start date yields
"started early"
→ proactivity), and one signal can roll into multiple metrics. Derivation rules can
also be parameterized by context, since what counts as "early" in a frantic startup environment
differs from what counts as "early" in an academic one.
3.2 Reward systems as lenses
A reward system is, almost exactly, a reward function: it defines what is valued in a context, how
raw behavior maps to value, and what counts as exceptional versus ordinary. Every individual
has a distinct profile in every reward system — like a player's stat card, which differs between
the Pokémon universe and the WWE universe.
Concretely, a reward system defines:
-
-
-
which metrics are even relevant or visible in this context;
how signals roll up into those metrics here (the measurement model);
the normalization baseline — what is "normal" versus "standout" in this population;
-
and the weighting — how much each metric matters.
Crucially, a reward system's relationship to a trait is a function, not a scalar weight. Many
traits are bipolar spectra ("high agency vs. high compliance,
" extrovert vs. introvert), not
more-is-better magnitudes. A startup context rewards the agency pole; a regulated-industry
context rewards the compliance pole; some traits are genuinely monotonic. So
trait_emphasis encodes a reward-over-trait curve — monotonic, peaked at an ideal point, or
pole-preferring. In the hiring use case,
"fit" then becomes the alignment between a candidate's
trait vector and the reward system's value function: two people with identical profiles can fit two
contexts very differently.
3.3 The canonical trait basis
The single decision that determines whether the system is buildable is this: do reward systems
define their metrics in entirely bespoke terms, or do they project onto a shared substrate? Fully
bespoke means transposition requires a hand-built mapping for every pair of systems —
unmaintainable. Fully universal means trivial transposition but no richness.
The answer is in between. We maintain a canonical trait basis: a stable, relatively small set of
latent dimensions (agency, conscientiousness, collaboration, creativity, resilience,
communication, risk appetite, learning velocity, and so on), ideally anchored to validated
psychometrics where they exist so we inherit their credibility. Each reward system is then a
projection onto that basis plus its own native metrics.
-
-
Native metrics are bespoke to a reward system — maximum fidelity inside it, but they
only travel via learned mappings. They are the system's "untranslatable loanwords.
"
Projected (canonical) metrics are the lossy-but-comparable representation that makes
any-to-any transposition possible.
The basis is the interlingua and the transposition hub — the reserve currency through which
profiles in different reward systems become commensurable.
4. Data ingestion: connectors and variable altitude
Because the interpretation ladder is explicit, connectors do not all enter at the same rung. Each
connector declares the altitude at which it feeds data:
-
-
-
A pure data source (e.g. an LMS exposing only timestamps) feeds at the observation
level, and the app runs its own derivations to manufacture the signals.
A richer source that already computes "submitted early" feeds at the signal level and
skips derivation.
Our own suite apps (project-based learning, mentorship) will typically feed signals
directly; a generic third-party plug-in may only manage raw observations.
Same pipeline, different entry points — the app fills in whatever lower layers are missing below
the declared altitude. This is what connector configuration actually negotiates: not just "what is
your schema,
" but "at what altitude are you handing me data, which derivations apply if it is raw,
and how do your fields map into my schema.
" During this configuration, the source shares
enough of its metric semantics and schema that the profile app can transpose it into the
canonical model.
5. Credentialing and trust
Data is only as valuable as it is verifiable, so credentialing is built into the source, not added
later. The layered model makes clear that trust lives at two distinct levels:
-
-
Trust in the observation — is the fact accurate, did the source faithfully report the real
submission date, and who attested it?
Trust in the derivation — is the logic that turned facts into an insight sound, and who
authored it?
When a connector feeds raw observations and our rules derive the signal, the whole chain is
transparent and auditable — we can show exactly how "reliability" was computed and from
what. When a connector hands over a pre-derived signal, we are trusting its derivation logic,
which we usually cannot inspect. Those two cases are not equivalent and should not be scored
the same. Every signal therefore records whether it was app-derived (with the rule and version,
and the observation IDs it came from) or externally asserted (opaque).
This yields a useful calibration behavior: when a connector supplies both the raw observations
and its own derived signal, the app can recompute with its own rule and compare. Agreement
raises our trust in that source; divergence flags it. Over time this tunes how much we believe
each connector's opaque assertions.
As part of configuring and piping data, a source can optionally specify who verified a given
metric and when — for example, a mentor attesting a student's punctuality.
6. Context and reward systems in depth
Context attaches at the point of capture — an observation is recorded inside a reward system or
one of its sub-contexts — and is preserved up the ladder. A generic aggregated metric such as
"agency,
" read outside its context, can be actively misleading, which is why a profile is never
shown in isolation of its reward system.
Reward systems are not flat; they compose. A "higher education" reward system can contain
"coursework,
" "internships,
" and "extracurricular leadership" sub-contexts, and the weights by
which those sub-contexts aggregate up the tree are themselves part of the parent system's
definition. Modeling this hierarchy from the start avoids retrofitting it later.
7. Transposition between reward systems
Transposing a profile from one reward system to another is currency conversion, and every
conversion carries a fidelity score. There are three routes, in increasing sophistication:
-
-
-
Through the canonical basis (hub-and-spoke). Project the source system's metrics
into the trait basis, then apply the target system's lens. Always available, always
somewhat lossy — the reserve-currency route.
Direct learned mapping (pairwise). When many individuals hold profiles in both
systems, learn a direct A→B mapping. More accurate where data is dense — the
direct-exchange-rate route.
Cold-start semantic mapping. When a brand-new connector plugs in with no
overlapping individuals, bootstrap the mapping from the metadata it shares during
configuration — schema plus semantic descriptions of what each metric means —
aligning its metrics to the canonical traits before a single shared person exists.
Adjacent contexts (two software-engineering reward systems) transpose at high confidence;
distant ones (Pokémon → WWE) transpose at low confidence, and we surface that honestly
rather than presenting the number as solid. Every transposed value is flagged as transposed
and carries its fidelity.
8. The anatomy of a metric
A metric is never just a number; it is a number plus its evidential context. For a given individual
in a given reward system, a metric reading carries:
-
-
-
-
-
-
-
Value and the signals it was derived from (full traceability down to observations).
Attestations — who verified it, in what role, when, and at what scope.
Verification level — self-reported, peer, mentor, institutional, or cryptographic.
Evidence density — was this one data point or a hundred?
Recency / decay — how current the reading is.
Statistical confidence — a confidence interval, and percentile within the population.
Transposition fidelity — whether the reading is native to this system or transposed in,
and how reliably.
These axes are largely orthogonal: a mentor-attested punctuality score built on three
observations is high-verification but low-density, and the experience must be able to tell those
apart.
9. User experience
Despite the depth beneath it, the surface must feel peppy, simple, and derived — a fitness app,
not a monitoring system. Each reward system surfaces a small number of confident headline
metrics, presented as progress and "leveling up" rather than as raw logs. The full provenance
chain — metric ← signal ← observations, each annotated with its rule and attestation — is
exactly what powers the drill-down: tap a ring, see the insight behind it, then see the raw facts it
came from. The richness is always available, but never in the user's face.
